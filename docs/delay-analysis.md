## The finding

Achalasia's diagnostic delay has been measured at least six times since 1997, in adults and children, in Germany, France, Italy and Iran. The answers range from a median of 10.5 months in French children to a mean of 5.7 years in French adults, and the largest recent study puts the median at two years.

For the other four conditions, no study was built to measure it. The one Zenker's figure, 17 months, is a side note in a 28-patient treatment series. The empty cells are the result.

R-CPD has no delay study either, but it has something achalasia does not: direct evidence that the system is not finding these patients at all. In the 5 studies in a 2025 systematic review that recorded how the diagnosis was made, 78.9% of patients recognised it themselves (Lechien 2025). In one French clinic, 105 of 106 did, after a combined 162 medical visits, and the gap between the average age at first symptoms and at diagnosis was about 17 years (Mailly 2025). Every patient in a 13-person interview study first learned the name on social media (Miller 2024).

So the two ends of the same tube look like this. Achalasia is slow to diagnose, but the diagnosis comes from doctors and its delay is measured. R-CPD is diagnosed mostly by patients, and its delay has never been measured.

## How sure

- The achalasia studies disagree with each other by years, partly because some report medians and some report means, and partly because they span 1980 to 2023.
- The R-CPD 17 years is two group averages from one specialist clinic, not each person's own wait.
- Papers that measure delay but do not use the words "diagnostic delay", "time to diagnosis" or "misdiagnosis" in their record could have been missed. The R-CPD abstracts were all read in full to reduce that risk for R-CPD.
